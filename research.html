Job Market Paper

The Effects of Corporate Purchase Programmes on Market Power

Draft (PDF)

Job market paper

Does unconventional monetary policy affect the market power of firms? I address this question both empirically and theoretically. Comparing firms purchased under the European Central Bank’s Corporate Sector Purchase Programme with eligible but unpurchased ones in a staggered difference-in-differences design, I show that a purchase raises a firm’s markups by 7.1 percent. The transmission channel runs from issuing conditions to capital: purchased bonds are issued at a 22 basis point lower yield to maturity, and purchased firms invest 2.2 percentage points more. These effects materialise only under two conditions: purchases must be repeated over the years, and they must happen within a week of the bond issuance. A general equilibrium model rationalises these findings, combining endogenous markups and financial frictions. In a segmented bond market, a purchase raises the price of the very issue it absorbs, relaxes the issuer’s capital constraint, and thus raises its output, market share and markup. A quantitative version compares different purchase designs: several, including the ECB’s own with its deviations from market neutrality, deliver almost the same welfare gain, while concentrating purchases on a few large firms or directing them to the smallest eligible ones reduces it.

Synthetic Risk Transfers: Effects on Financial Fragility and Redistribution — with Gabriela Araujo

Sovereign Spreads, Cross-Country Redistribution and Quantitative Easing in a Monetary Union — with Antonio Giribaldi and Iginio Marchesini Reggiani

In a monetary union without a fiscal union, each government pays its own interest rate while its bonds are held across borders. Spread differentials that arise from financial frictions rather than default risk therefore redistribute resources between the households of different member states, a channel distinct from the level of interest rates. We document the interest flows that spread differentials generate across euro-area countries, using bilateral holdings imputed from the ECB’s Securities Holdings Statistics. We then build a two-country New Keynesian model in which financial frictions generate these differentials. After a common financial shock, the spread of the more indebted country rises more, and resources flow from its households to those of the other country. Quantitative easing allocated by the capital key barely narrows the differential and leaves most of this redistribution in place; purchases targeted at the high-spread country close the differential and largely offset it.
